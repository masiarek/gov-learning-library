# The Articles of Confederation

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** America's first constitution, deliberately built too weak to become a tyranny — and, it turned out, too weak to govern.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it was** | The first constitution of the United States. **Drafted and adopted by Congress in November 1777**; **ratified and in force 1 March 1781**; replaced by the Constitution in 1789. One of the [nine required founding documents](../../FOUNDING_DOCUMENTS.md). |
| **The design** | A **confederal** system: a single-chamber Congress in which each state had **one vote** regardless of size, no executive, no national judiciary. States kept "sovereignty, freedom and independence." |
| **The thresholds** | **Nine of thirteen** states to pass a major measure (war, treaties, coinage, borrowing) — the "2/3" of the notes. **All thirteen** to amend anything. |
| **Why it matters** | Every major feature of the Constitution is a direct answer to something that failed here. You cannot explain 1787 without it. |
| **Common mistake** | Reading it as simple incompetence. Almost every weakness was **deliberate** — the drafters had just fought a war against a strong central government and were not about to build another one. It failed at the thing it was designed not to do. |

## From the class notes

> **PURPOSE OF THE AOC:** leave state sovereignty in place · legislation passed w/ a 2/3 vote in congress (one vote per state) · amendments need 100% support from congress & state legislatures · Money can't be taken forcibly from the states · A standing Army can't be raised w/o support from states. Made due to a fear of a tyrannical government.
>
> **PROBLEMS: WEAK.** no central leadership · congress has no power to enforce laws · can't regulate trade · no court system · changes required unanimous consent from all 13 states […] Nothing got done.

This is a strong set of notes — the "made due to a fear of a tyrannical government" line is the key insight, and both voting thresholds are right.

### ⚠ Sharpening — the date, and two details

**"Created in 1781"** is the *ratification* date. The Articles were **drafted and adopted by Congress in November 1777** and took until **1 March 1781** to come into force. Maryland was the last holdout, and its reason mattered: it refused to ratify until states with colonial charters claiming land to the Pacific — Virginia above all — gave up those western claims to the nation. That fight over the west is what produced the Northwest Ordinance a few years later.

**"No court system"** is right where it counts — no national judiciary. Article IX did let Congress establish courts for piracy and felonies on the high seas, and made Congress the last resort in **state-versus-state** disputes. That state-versus-state problem is a better example than the notes' "if states have an issue w/ the fed gov", since suing the government was not a live concept yet.

**"Each state has their own constitution and Bill of Rights"** — most did. **Connecticut and Rhode Island** kept modified colonial charters rather than writing new constitutions, and not every state attached a separate declaration of rights. → [full corrections](../../CORRECTIONS.md#dates-and-precision)

## The full picture

### What Congress could and could not do

| **Could** | **Could not** |
|---|---|
| Declare war and make peace | **Tax.** It could only *requisition* states — ask, with no way to compel |
| Make treaties and exchange ambassadors | **Regulate trade**, between states or with foreign nations |
| Borrow money and issue bills of credit | **Enforce** any law it passed, or any treaty it signed |
| Settle disputes between states | Raise troops directly — it requisitioned them from states too |
| Run a post office, set weights and measures | Stop states issuing their own money |
| Manage relations with Native nations | Amend itself without unanimous consent |

Notice the shape: everything in the left column depends on the states voluntarily cooperating, and there is nothing in the right column to make them.

### The failures, concretely

**No taxing power.** Congress requisitioned about $10 million from the states between 1781 and 1786 and received roughly a **fifth** of it. Soldiers went unpaid; war debt to France, the Netherlands and domestic lenders went unserviced. In 1783 unpaid officers at Newburgh came close to a mutiny, defused only by Washington personally.

**No commerce power.** States taxed each other's goods. New York taxed firewood from Connecticut and vegetables from New Jersey; states with good harbours taxed the trade of states without. Nine states built their own navies. Britain, meanwhile, could sign a trade deal with one state and cut out the rest, because there was no national counterparty.

**No enforcement.** The Treaty of Paris (1783) obliged Americans to repay pre-war debts to British creditors and to stop confiscating loyalist property. States ignored both, so Britain kept its forts in the Northwest in retaliation, and Congress could do nothing about either side of it.

**Unanimity for amendment.** Everyone could see the tax problem. A national **5% import duty** was proposed twice, in 1781 and 1783, and both times **twelve** states agreed — blocked first by Rhode Island, then by New York. Two states out of thirteen could, and did, block the fix.

### What it actually got right — do not skip this

The notes are all failures, which is the standard telling, but the Confederation government also:

- **won the Revolutionary War**, and
- **negotiated the Treaty of Paris (1783)** on strikingly good terms, and
- passed the **Land Ordinance of 1785**, which set up the rectangular survey system that still shapes the map of the Midwest, and
- passed the **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** — the most consequential law of the era. It banned slavery in the Northwest Territory, guaranteed religious freedom and trial by jury there, funded public schools, and above all established that new territories would enter the Union as **full and equal states** rather than as colonies.

That last point deserves weight. Britain's inability to imagine colonies as equals is what caused the Revolution; the Confederation Congress, at its weakest, wrote the opposite principle into law and the United States has expanded by it ever since.

### How it ended

[Shays' Rebellion](../shays_rebellion/README.md) (1786–87) was the trigger, but the machinery was already moving. The **Annapolis Convention** of September 1786 was called to discuss interstate trade; only five states sent delegates, too few to do anything. Alexander Hamilton and James Madison used the failure to issue a bolder call: a convention at Philadelphia in May 1787 to consider changes to the Articles.

That convention was authorised only to *revise* the Articles. It threw them out and wrote a new constitution instead — and, knowing the unanimity rule made amendment impossible, wrote into Article VII that the new document would take effect on ratification by **nine** states, not thirteen. Strictly speaking, that was illegal under the Articles. It is also the only reason there is a Constitution.

**Where it sits in AP.** A required document, and the whole of **Topic 1.4 (Challenges of the Articles of Confederation)**. The exam pattern is: name a weakness, name the constitutional provision that fixed it. Worth building that table yourself — no tax power → Article I §8 taxing clause; no commerce power → the commerce clause; no enforcement → an executive and a federal judiciary; unanimity → Article V's three-quarters rule.
