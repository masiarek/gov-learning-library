# Classifications of Government

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Three ways to answer one question — *where does sovereignty sit?* — with the central government, with the member states, or divided between them.

| | |
|---|---|
| **The question** | Not "who is in charge day to day", but who holds **sovereignty**: the final authority that is not delegated from anyone else. |
| **Unitary** | The central government holds sovereignty. Local units exist because the centre created them, exercise powers **delegated** from it, and can have those powers changed or withdrawn. |
| **Confederal** | The **member states** hold sovereignty. The central body has only the powers the states have granted it, acts on the states rather than on citizens, and can be reined in by them. |
| **Federal** | Sovereignty is **divided** between a national and a regional level by a written constitution. Both levels act directly on the same citizens; neither can unilaterally abolish the other. |
| **Common mistake** | Equating unitary with tyranny. The UK, France, Japan, and every U.S. **state** relative to its own counties and cities are unitary — and are not tyrannies. Unitary describes where power *sits*, not how it is *used*. |

## From the class notes

> **UNITARY:** one body rules. Worries about too much power in one gov causing corruption, may cause an oppressive king. ⚠
> **CONFEDERATE:** power resides within smaller states / localized gov.
> **FEDERAL:** power split between bodies.

Confederal and federal are right. The unitary line needs work.

### ⚠ Correction — that is the *fear*, not the definition

"May cause an oppressive king" describes what the founders were afraid of, which is a real and relevant thing to know — but it is not what unitary *means*. The **United Kingdom, France and Japan are unitary democracies**. So, closer to home, is every American state: your county and your city have no sovereignty of their own, only what the state legislature grants them, and the state can restructure or abolish them. That principle even has a name — **Dillon's Rule**.

Better definition: *the central government holds the sovereignty; any local units exercise power delegated from it, and it can take that power back.* Then add the founders' fear as a separate note, because it explains why they refused the model. → [full correction](../../CORRECTIONS.md#dates-and-precision)

## The full picture

### Side by side

| | **Unitary** | **Confederal** | **Federal** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sovereignty sits with | the central government | the member states | both, divided by constitution |
| Local/state powers come from | delegation by the centre | their own sovereignty | the constitution itself |
| The central government acts on | citizens directly | the member states | citizens directly |
| Can the other level be abolished? | yes — centre may restructure locals | yes — states may leave or dissolve it | no — neither can abolish the other |
| Examples | UK, France, Japan, China; every U.S. state over its localities | Articles of Confederation; Confederate States; the UN and (partly) the EU | United States, Germany, Canada, Australia, India, Mexico, Brazil |

### The spectrum is the point

The three are not a random list — they are one dial, from all power at the centre to all power at the edges, and the American story is a deliberate walk along it:

**Britain (unitary) → the Articles (confederal) → the Constitution (federal).**

The colonies had just escaped a unitary system in which a distant central authority claimed the right to legislate for them "in all cases whatsoever". So they overcorrected, hard, into a confederation that could not tax, could not regulate trade, and could not enforce anything. [When that failed](../articles_of_confederation/README.md), federalism was the compromise — and it was a genuine invention. There was no working model of it in 1787.

That is what James Madison means when he calls the Constitution neither wholly national nor wholly federal in Federalist 39. It was strange enough at the time to need explaining.

### What makes federalism actually work

Two features do the real work, and both are worth memorising because Unit 1's later topics turn on them:

1. **Both levels act directly on citizens.** Under the Articles, Congress could only ask *states* for money and soldiers, and had no way to compel a state that said no. Under the Constitution the national government taxes you, drafts you, and prosecutes you — no state has to agree. This is the single biggest structural change of 1787.
2. **The division is entrenched in a written constitution**, interpreted by courts. Neither Congress nor the states can unilaterally rewrite the boundary. The **Tenth Amendment** reserves undelegated powers to the states; the **supremacy clause** (Article VI) makes federal law win where they conflict; and where exactly the line falls has been argued in the Supreme Court ever since.

### Don't confuse it with separation of powers

These are two different divisions and the exam likes to test whether you can keep them apart:

- **Federalism** divides power **by level** — national vs state. Vertical.
- **Separation of powers** divides power **by function** — legislative, executive, judicial. Horizontal.

The Constitution does both at once, which is the "double security" Madison advertises in **Federalist 51**.
