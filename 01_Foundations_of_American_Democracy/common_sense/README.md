# Common Sense

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** A 47-page pamphlet, published anonymously in January 1776, that turned independence from a radical idea into an obvious one — by attacking not the King's policies but monarchy itself.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | A political pamphlet by **Thomas Paine**, published in Philadelphia on **10 January 1776**, anonymously ("written by an Englishman"). |
| **The argument** | Reconciliation is impossible and undesirable. Hereditary monarchy is absurd in principle — no one earns a right to rule by being born. An island cannot permanently govern a continent. America's trade interests are hurt, not helped, by being dragged into Britain's European wars. Independence, and a republic. |
| **Why it worked** | It was written for **ordinary readers** — plain words, Biblical references, no Latin, no legal citations — and sold for two shillings. Estimates of circulation in 1776 run from 100,000 upward, in a colonial population of about 2.5 million. It was read aloud in taverns and to troops. |
| **Why it matters** | It changed *what people thought could be said*. Before it, the public argument was about rights within the empire; after it, independence was a mainstream position. The Declaration follows six months later. |
| **Common mistake** | Spelling: **Paine**, not Payne. And it is not a government document — it has no legal force whatever. Its power was entirely persuasive. |

## From the class notes

> **COMMON SENSE** — Pamphlet written by Thomas Payne ⚠ — Called for independence and formation of a democratic republic.

### ⚠ Correction — Thomas **Paine**

Not "Payne". → [full correction](../../CORRECTIONS.md#5-thomas-paine)

The rest of the note is right, with one refinement worth making: Paine argued for a **republic**, and specifically against monarchy and hereditary rule. "Democratic republic" is a fair modern label for what he wanted — he proposed frequent elections and a large, single-chamber assembly — but *republic* is the word he and his contemporaries used, and the word the exam uses.

## The full picture

### Who Paine was

He arrived in Philadelphia in **November 1774**, aged 37, with a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin and a record of failure behind him: corset-maker, sailor, twice a tax officer and twice dismissed, once bankrupt, once widowed and once separated. He had been in America **fourteen months** when he published the pamphlet that helped start a revolution.

That biography is part of why the pamphlet reads the way it does. Paine had no property, no legal training and no social position to protect, so he had no reason to write like the lawyers and planters who dominated colonial politics — and no stake in the elaborate constitutional argument they had been making about the rights of Englishmen.

### The move that mattered

Every other Patriot writer attacked **Parliament** and preserved the King, on the theory that the colonies were loyal subjects wronged by a corrupt ministry. Paine attacked **the King, and monarchy as an institution**. He called George III "the Royal Brute of Britain" and argued from scripture that hereditary rule was against God's design.

Once that argument lands, reconciliation has nothing left to reconcile *to*. You cannot ask a legitimate sovereign for redress if you have just denied that any hereditary sovereign is legitimate. Paine did not persuade people that independence was achievable; he removed the reason to want anything else.

He also made a positive case that shows up in the Declaration's structure: government exists only as "a necessary evil" to secure people's safety and rights, so its form is a practical question for the living, not an inheritance.

### Afterwards

Paine gave up his royalties. He went on to write **The American Crisis** (December 1776 — "These are the times that try men's souls"), which Washington had read to his troops before Trenton; then **Rights of Man** (1791) defending the French Revolution, which got him convicted of seditious libel in absentia in Britain; then **The Age of Reason**, a deist attack on organised religion that cost him most of his American reputation. Six people attended his funeral in 1809.

**Where it sits in AP.** *Common Sense* is **not** one of the [nine required founding documents](../../FOUNDING_DOCUMENTS.md) and will not appear as a document-based question. It belongs to **Topic 1.2** as context: the popular argument for the natural-rights, consent-based government the Declaration then asserts.
