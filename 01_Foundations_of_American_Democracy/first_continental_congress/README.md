# The First Continental Congress

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Twelve colonies sent delegates to Philadelphia to answer the Intolerable Acts together — and in doing so acted, for the first time, like a single political body.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it was** | A convention of **56 delegates from 12 of the 13 colonies**, meeting at Carpenters' Hall in Philadelphia from **5 September to 26 October 1774**. |
| **Why it met** | To respond jointly to the **Coercive (Intolerable) Acts**, which Parliament had aimed at Massachusetts alone. |
| **What it did** | Adopted the **Declaration and Resolves** (14 October) stating colonial rights, and the **Continental Association** (20 October) — a binding boycott of British goods, enforced locally by committees. |
| **Why it matters** | It had no legal authority of any kind. It acted anyway, and the colonies obeyed. That is the first working demonstration that a *continental* government was possible — the direct ancestor of the Second Continental Congress, the Articles, and Congress itself. |
| **Common mistake** | It did **not** declare independence, and most delegates did not want it. The Congress explicitly affirmed loyalty to the Crown; it was asking for the pre-1763 relationship back. Independence is still two years away. |

Established a trade boycott against Great Britain.

All correct, including all three numbers — and the self-correction from "states" to "colonies" is the right instinct: in 1774 there were no states.

## The full picture

### Why Georgia stayed home

Georgia was the youngest, smallest and most exposed colony. It was in active conflict with the Creek on its frontier and depended on British troops and subsidies to hold the line; its royal governor, James Wright, also worked hard to block the colony from sending anyone. Georgia joined the **Second** Continental Congress in 1775.

### What the Congress actually agreed

The delegates split between those wanting reconciliation and those wanting confrontation. What emerged split the difference and, in practice, favoured the confrontational side.

**The Declaration and Resolves** (also called the Declaration of Rights and Grievances) listed the colonists' rights as English subjects — to "life, liberty and property", to assembly and petition, to trial by jury of the vicinage, to be taxed only by their own assemblies — and named thirteen acts of Parliament since 1763 as violations. It stopped short of denying Parliament's authority entirely, conceding it could regulate external trade.

**The Continental Association** was the teeth. Non-importation of British goods from December 1774, non-consumption from March 1775, non-exportation to Britain from September 1775 if the acts were not repealed. Crucially, it was enforced **from below**: every county, city and town was to elect a committee of inspection to publish the names of violators. Thousands of ordinary colonists ended up serving on those committees — an unplanned mass political education, and in many places the committees quietly became the real local government.

The delegates also agreed to reconvene in May 1775 if nothing changed. Nothing changed. By the time the **Second Continental Congress** met on 10 May 1775, Lexington and Concord had already been fought (19 April 1775) — so the body that assembled to petition the King found itself running a war instead.

### The people in the room

The notes' list is a good one. Worth adding what each is *for* on an exam: **George Washington** (Virginia) — the man the Second Congress will make commander-in-chief; **Patrick Henry** (Virginia) — the radical voice, who told the Congress "I am not a Virginian, but an American"; **John Adams** (Massachusetts) — later on the committee that drafts the Declaration; **Samuel Adams** (Massachusetts) — the Sons of Liberty organiser who did more than anyone to bring the crisis to this point. **Peyton Randolph** of Virginia presided.

**A note on the abbreviation.** The notes write **FCC** for First Continental Congress. Fine as private shorthand, but do not use it on an exam — outside this notebook FCC is the Federal Communications Commission, which turns up in Unit 2. Write it out.

**Where it sits in AP.** Background to **Topic 1.2**. The exam does not test the Congress's proceedings, but the pattern it establishes — colonies acting jointly through delegates, with no central executive and no power to compel anyone — is exactly the design that becomes [the Articles of Confederation](../articles_of_confederation/README.md).
