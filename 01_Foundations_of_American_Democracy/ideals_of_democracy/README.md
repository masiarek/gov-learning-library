# Ideals of Democracy

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Five Enlightenment ideas — natural rights, popular sovereignty, social contract, republicanism, limited government — that the Declaration asserts and the Constitution builds machinery for.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Natural rights** | Rights every human being has simply by being human. They are **not granted by government**, so government cannot legitimately take them away. Locke: life, liberty, property. Jefferson: life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. |
| **Popular sovereignty** | Ultimate political authority rests with the people. Government is created by them and answerable to them. "We the People." |
| **Social contract** | People give up some absolute freedom to a government in exchange for protection of their rights. Break the bargain and the people may replace the government. |
| **Republicanism** | The people rule **through elected representatives** rather than directly. |
| **Limited government** | Government's power is restricted — by a written constitution, by law, and by structure. It may do only what it is authorised to do. |
| **Common mistake** | Reciting them as five separate vocabulary words. They are one **argument**, in order, and the exam rewards students who can run the chain. |

## From the class notes

> **LIMITED GOVERNMENT, NATURAL RIGHTS, POPULAR SOVEREIGNTY, REPUBLICANISM, & SOCIAL CONTRACT** are core ideals of American democracy, which come from Enlightenment thinkers and shape our foundational documents.

That opening line is exactly the College Board's five, correctly named. The individual definitions that follow are all sound too — "give up something, get something else in return" is a better one-line statement of the social contract than most textbooks manage.

### ⚠ Correction — natural rights are *human* rights

The notes read: "There are rights important for all humans **and animals** to have out of natural law." Drop the animals.

In Locke's *Second Treatise* and in the Declaration that borrows from it, natural rights belong to human beings **by virtue of being human** — the argument runs through reason, moral agency, and the capacity to give or withhold consent, none of which the tradition extends to animals. Animal rights is a real and serious area of moral philosophy, but a different one, and it arrives roughly two centuries later.

It matters here because the entire force of the concept is in the **source**: rights you hold *prior to* and *independent of* any government are rights no government can legitimately remove — which is what licenses the right of revolution. Widening the category blurs the argument the Declaration is making.

The second half of the note — **"Life, liberty, and pursuit of happiness (previously property)"** — is exactly right, and is the detail most students miss. → [full correction](../../CORRECTIONS.md#3-natural-rights-are-human-rights)

## The full picture

### Run them as one argument

Do not memorise five definitions. Memorise the chain, because it is how the Declaration's second paragraph is actually built:

1. **Natural rights.** People have rights before any government exists.
2. **Social contract.** To secure those rights, they consent to be governed, surrendering some freedom in return for protection.
3. **Popular sovereignty.** So the government's authority is *derived* — it comes from the people, and belongs to nobody by birth or force.
4. **Republicanism.** In practice the people exercise that authority through elected representatives rather than in person.
5. **Limited government.** Since the authority was granted for a purpose, it extends only that far. Exceed it, and the contract is broken.

Then the conclusion the whole chain exists to license: a government that breaks the contract may be **altered or abolished**. That is the Declaration's argument, and it is why these five ideas appear in this course at all.

### Where each one is written down

| Ideal | In the Declaration | In the Constitution |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural rights** | "endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights, that among these are Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" | The **Bill of Rights** — phrased as restrictions on government ("Congress shall make no law…"), not as grants to citizens. The phrasing is the point. |
| **Popular sovereignty** | "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed" | "**We the People** of the United States … do ordain and establish this Constitution." Elections; **Article V** amendment. |
| **Social contract** | "That to secure these rights, Governments are instituted among Men" | The whole document is the written terms. The **Preamble** states the purposes for which power is granted. |
| **Republicanism** | (implicit — the colonies act through Congress) | Elected House, Senate and President; **Article IV §4** guarantees every state "a Republican Form of Government". |
| **Limited government** | "whenever any Form of Government becomes destructive of these ends…" | **Enumerated powers** (Article I §8); **separation of powers**; **checks and balances**; **Tenth Amendment**; the Bill of Rights. |

### The thinkers behind them

**John Locke** (1632–1704), *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), is the main source. In the state of nature people are free and equal and hold natural rights to life, liberty and property; the inconvenience of enforcing those rights themselves leads them to form a government by consent; a government that violates the trust may be resisted. Jefferson is working directly from this — closely enough that his contemporaries noticed.

**Thomas Hobbes** (*Leviathan*, 1651) shares the social-contract method and reaches an opposite conclusion. His state of nature is a war of all against all in which life is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short, so people trade nearly all their freedom for an absolute sovereign — and once the bargain is made there is **no right to take it back**. The contrast is the most useful thing you can hold about either man: same premise, same tool, opposite result, because they start from different assumptions about human nature. The founders chose Locke.

**Baron de Montesquieu** (*The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748) supplies what Locke does not: **separation of powers** into legislative, executive and judicial branches, so that power checks power. The structural half of limited government is his, and the founders cite him constantly.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762) — "man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains" — pushes toward popular sovereignty exercised *directly* by the people through a general will. He is the direct-democracy strand, and the framers were wary of him for exactly the reasons in [Federalist 10](../types_of_democracy/README.md).

### The gap the exam expects you to name

"All men are created equal" was written by a man who enslaved more than six hundred people over his life, in a document adopted by a Congress that struck out its own condemnation of the slave trade, creating a country in which women could not vote and property qualifications limited the franchise for white men too.

The productive way to hold this — and the way the College Board frames it — is that the ideals were stated **more broadly than they were practised**, and that the subsequent history of American politics is substantially the story of people demanding that the practice be brought up to the statement. Frederick Douglass, Elizabeth Cady Stanton at Seneca Falls, and Martin Luther King Jr. in the **Letter from a Birmingham Jail** — a [required document](../../FOUNDING_DOCUMENTS.md) — all argue in exactly that form: not that the founding ideals are wrong, but that America should be made to keep them.

**Where it sits in AP.** **Topic 1.1 (Ideals of Democracy)** — the first topic of the course. The required document is the **Declaration of Independence**, and the expected skill is quoting its language as evidence for a named ideal.
