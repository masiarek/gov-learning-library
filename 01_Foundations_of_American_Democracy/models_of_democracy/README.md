# Models of Democracy

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Three competing answers to "who actually decides in the United States" — everyone (participatory), organised groups (pluralist), or a small elite — each of which can point to real features of the Constitution as support.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Participatory** | Broad, direct citizen involvement in politics and civil society. Democracy is something you *do*, not something you delegate. |
| **Pluralist** | Policy emerges from competition and bargaining among many **organised interest groups**. No single group dominates; power is dispersed among them. |
| **Elite** | A small, wealthy, well-educated minority holds disproportionate influence. Ordinary citizens participate mainly by choosing between elites at elections. |
| **How they relate** | They are **descriptive models**, not systems of government — three lenses on the same country. All three are partly right, which is why all three survive. |
| **Common mistake** | Treating them as three *kinds* of democracy you could pick between, like direct and representative. They are competing explanations of one system. And the exam almost never asks for the definition alone — it asks which **constitutional provision** supports which model. |

## From the class notes

> Three main models (theories) of democracy that explain how power and influence operate in the U.S.
>
> **1. PARTICIPATORY** — emphasizes direct citizen involvement […] ex: Town hall meetings, ballot initiatives, protests, voting, funding causes
> **2. PLURALIST** — Recognizes group-based activism by non-governmental interests striving for an impact on political decisions […] ex: Interest groups, labor unions.
> **3. ELITE** — Emphasizes limited citizen participation […] A small number of people, usually wealthy and well educated, have the most power. Prior to the 17th amendment, the senate was believed to have the most power — they were educated, wealthy white men who weren't elected by the people.

**No corrections needed on this page.** All three descriptions are sound, the examples are the standard ones, and the 17th Amendment point is exactly right — before 1913 senators were chosen by state legislatures, not by voters, which is the cleanest constitutional evidence for the elite model there is.

What follows is expansion, not correction: the piece the notes are missing is the *link to the documents*, which is what the exam actually asks for.

## The full picture

### The move the exam is really testing

The College Board rarely asks "define pluralist democracy." It asks you to **tie a model to a constitutional provision or a founding document**. So learn each model with its evidence attached:

| Model | Constitutional / documentary support |
|---|---|
| **Participatory** | The **First Amendment** rights to assemble and to petition. The suffrage amendments — **15th** (race), **17th** (direct election of senators), **19th** (sex), **24th** (poll taxes), **26th** (age 18) — each of which widened who may participate. State-level initiative and referendum. |
| **Pluralist** | The **First Amendment** rights of association and petition, which make interest groups possible at all. **Federalist 10**'s "multiplicity of interests" — Madison's argument that many competing factions in a large republic check one another. |
| **Elite** | The **Electoral College**. The **Senate before the 17th Amendment**, chosen by state legislatures. The **federal judiciary** — appointed, not elected, and serving for life. **Brutus 1**'s warning that representatives in so large a republic will inevitably be the wealthy and well-born. |

### Federalist 10 supports two of them at once

This is the subtle bit, and it is worth getting right because it makes you sound like you understand the document rather than a summary of it.

Madison's argument that a large republic contains so many competing factions that none can dominate is the **pluralist** case, made two centuries before anyone used the word. But in the same paper he says representation will "refine and enlarge the public views" by passing them through "a chosen body of citizens, whose wisdom may best discern the true interest of their country" — the idea that the filtered judgment of a selected few is *better* than raw public opinion. That is the **elite** case.

Both readings are legitimately in the text. A free-response answer that notices this is stronger than one that files Federalist 10 under a single label.

### The theorists behind the names

**Pluralism** is associated above all with **Robert Dahl**, whose *Who Governs?* (1961) studied who actually made decisions in New Haven, Connecticut. He found no single ruling group: different people held sway over education, over urban renewal, over party nominations, with little overlap. His conclusion was that American democracy is a **polyarchy** — rule by many minorities, none of them able to dominate the rest.

The **elite** model has an older pedigree — Mosca, Pareto, Michels — and its sharpest American statement is **C. Wright Mills**'s *The Power Elite* (1956), which argued that the commanding heights of the corporate world, the military and the executive branch are held by an interlocking group with shared background and interests. More recent quantitative work by **Gilens and Page** (2014) found that policy outcomes track the preferences of affluent Americans and organised business groups far more closely than those of the average citizen — the same claim, tested with data.

**Participatory** democracy as a named idea comes from the 1960s New Left, and specifically the **Port Huron Statement** (1962), which called for a society where individuals share in the social decisions that shape their lives. Its intellectual ancestors run back through Rousseau, and through Tocqueville's observation that Americans join associations for everything.

### The honest assessment

None of the three is simply correct, which is why the course teaches all three rather than the right one.

Pluralism's weakness is that **not everyone gets organised**. Forming an effective interest group takes money, time and expertise, so business and professional interests are represented far more thoroughly than the poor, the young, or the diffuse public. E. E. Schattschneider put it best: the flaw in the pluralist heaven is that the chorus sings with an upper-class accent.

Elite theory's weakness is that it struggles to explain real change driven from below — the abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, the Civil Rights Act, the collapse of the smoking industry's political power. If a stable elite always wins, those should not have happened.

Participatory theory's weakness is empirical: turnout in American midterm elections runs around 40–50%, and in local elections often far lower. A model of democracy that depends on broad engagement has to reckon with the fact that most people, most of the time, do not engage.

**Where it sits in AP.** **Topic 1.2 (Types of Democracy)**, alongside [direct versus representative](../types_of_democracy/README.md). Required documents: **Federalist 10**, **Brutus 1**, and the **Constitution**.
