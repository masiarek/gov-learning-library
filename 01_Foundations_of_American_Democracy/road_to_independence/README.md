# The Road to Independence

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Britain won a war, went deep into debt paying for it, decided the colonies should help — and discovered the colonies did not think Parliament had the right to ask.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What happened** | Between 1763 and 1775 Britain imposed a run of new taxes and controls on colonies that had been governing themselves in practice for over a century. The colonies answered with petitions, boycotts, and eventually organised destruction of property. |
| **The mechanism** | Each new tax produced a colonial objection *on principle*; each objection produced a harsher British response; each harsh response converted more moderates. The escalation is the story, not any single act. |
| **The slogan** | **"No taxation without representation."** Not a complaint that taxes were high — an argument that a legislature you have no part in electing has no authority to tax you at all. |
| **Why it matters** | Everything in the Declaration follows from this. The colonists are not arguing that the King is unkind; they are arguing that his government has lost its *legitimacy* — that it is taking property without consent. That is a natural-rights argument, and it needs the whole philosophical apparatus of [Unit 1's ideals](../ideals_of_democracy/README.md). |
| **Common mistake** | Thinking it was about money. British subjects at home paid **far higher** taxes than the colonists did, and the Tea Act actually *lowered* the price of tea. The fight was over who has the right to decide. |

## From the class notes

> **PRE REVOLUTION: TENSIONS RISE.** Tensions build between the British and American colonists following the French and Indian war. […] England beats France · England has a great amount of land in America · Has high debt from fighting an overseas war, needs colonies to pay some of it.

That three-line summary of the war's results is exactly right, and it is the right place to start — the debt is the cause of everything that follows.

## The full picture

### The war that started it

The **French and Indian War** (1754–1763) was the North American theatre of the Seven Years' War. Britain won decisively: the **Treaty of Paris (1763)** handed it Canada and everything France held east of the Mississippi.

It also roughly **doubled the British national debt**. And holding the new territory meant stationing troops in America permanently. From London the conclusion looked obvious — the colonies were the chief beneficiaries of an expensive victory, so the colonies should pay a share of the upkeep.

Two things made that reasoning explode. First, the **Proclamation of 1763** barred colonists from settling west of the Appalachians, which is where the land they had just helped win actually was. Second, a century of **salutary neglect** — Britain leaving colonial assemblies largely alone — meant the colonies had a working habit of taxing themselves through their own legislatures. What London called a reasonable request looked, from Boston, like a new and unconstitutional claim of power.

### The taxes, in order

| Year | Act | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1764 | **Sugar Act** | Duty on molasses and sugar, with stricter enforcement in vice-admiralty courts (no jury). |
| 1765 | **Stamp Act** | A tax stamp required on every legal document, newspaper, pamphlet, licence, and deck of cards. The first *direct, internal* tax on the colonies — and it touched exactly the professions best equipped to complain: lawyers, printers, merchants. |
| 1765 | **Quartering Act** | Colonies required to house and supply British troops. |
| 1767 | **Townshend Acts** | Duties on glass, lead, paint, paper and tea, plus a new customs board in Boston. |
| 1773 | **Tea Act** | Gave the East India Company a monopoly on colonial tea and *lowered* its price — while keeping the Townshend tea duty in place. |

### The argument underneath: virtual vs actual representation

Parliament's answer to "no taxation without representation" was **virtual representation** — the doctrine that every member of Parliament represents the interests of all British subjects everywhere, not just the voters of his own constituency. Whole English cities like Manchester elected nobody either, and were considered represented all the same.

The colonists rejected it and demanded **actual representation**: consent given by legislators the taxed people themselves elect. This is the real intellectual fault line of the whole crisis, and it never got resolved — which is why it ended in a war rather than a compromise.

### The three acts of civil disobedience

**The Sons of Liberty** (formed 1765, in response to the Stamp Act) were a network of local groups, most famously Boston's — Samuel Adams, John Hancock, Paul Revere among them. Their method was coercion short of killing: crowds, effigies, property destruction, and threats that persuaded tax officials to resign. Boston's stamp distributor, Andrew Oliver, quit in August 1765 after a mob hanged him in effigy and pulled down a building he owned. Their organising work is what made a *coordinated* colonial response possible at all.

**The Boston Massacre** (5 March 1770). A crowd harassed the lone sentry at the Custom House; Captain Thomas Preston brought reinforcements; a soldier knocked down by a thrown object fired, and others followed. **Five colonists died** — Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray and James Caldwell at the scene, Samuel Maverick the next morning, Patrick Carr two weeks later — and **six were wounded**. Whether anyone ordered "fire" was disputed then and is disputed now.

Two details the notes leave out, both worth knowing:

- **Paul Revere's engraving** of the scene — soldiers in a neat firing line, an officer clearly giving the order — was propaganda, not reportage. Calling it a "massacre" was itself a choice. This is the earliest good example in the course of political communication shaping public opinion.
- **John Adams defended the soldiers in court**, and won: six acquitted, two convicted only of manslaughter. He later called it one of the best things he ever did. He was a leading Patriot; he took the case anyway, because he thought a fair trial mattered more than the cause. It is the cleanest illustration of **rule of law** in the whole unit.

**The Boston Tea Party** (16 December 1773). Men associated with the Sons of Liberty, some disguised as Mohawks, boarded three ships and destroyed **342 chests** of East India Company tea. They damaged nothing else — the point was precision, not riot.

Britain's response was the **Coercive Acts** of 1774, which the colonists renamed the **Intolerable Acts**: the port of Boston closed until the tea was paid for; the Massachusetts charter rewritten to gut self-government; royal officials accused of crimes moved to England for trial; quartering extended. Parliament meant to isolate Massachusetts. Instead the other colonies read the acts as a template for what could be done to any of them — and called the [First Continental Congress](../first_continental_congress/README.md).

**Where it sits in AP.** Background rather than a tested topic — the exam starts at the Declaration. But the "no taxation without representation" argument *is* the natural-rights and consent-of-the-governed reasoning of **Topic 1.1**, arriving in its concrete form, and the escalation explains why the framers were so preoccupied with limiting power.
