# Shays' Rebellion

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Indebted farmers — many of them unpaid war veterans — shut down Massachusetts courts and marched on a federal arsenal, and the national government discovered it had no way to stop them.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What happened** | An armed uprising in western Massachusetts, **August 1786 – June 1787**, led in part by **Daniel Shays**, a farmer and former Continental Army captain. |
| **Why** | Massachusetts raised taxes sharply — payable in scarce **hard currency** — to pay off its war debt at full face value. Farmers holding depreciated paper faced foreclosure and debtors' prison. Petitions to the legislature failed. |
| **What they did** | Closed county courts to stop foreclosure proceedings; in January 1787 about 1,500 men attempted to seize the **Springfield Armory**. |
| **Who stopped it** | A **4,400-man state militia** under General **Benjamin Lincoln**, **privately financed by Boston merchants** — because Congress had tried and failed to raise a national force. |
| **Why it matters** | It converted a slow argument about the Articles into an emergency. It is the reason the Philadelphia Convention had the attendance and the mandate it did. |
| **Common mistake** | The spelling. **Shays'**, not "Shay's" — his name is Daniel **Shays**, with an s, so the possessive takes the apostrophe after it. |

## From the class notes

> Farmers turned soldiers turned farmers, unpaid for war. **DANIEL SHAYS:** Farmer; served in the American revolution. Higher taxes than ever, no way to pay — Shays & pals revolt. No government to stop the rebellion — weakness in the AoC is revealed. Wealthy people hired mercenaries to stop it. Shays and troops were eventually pardoned.

"Farmers turned soldiers turned farmers, unpaid for war" is a genuinely good line — it is the whole grievance in seven words. And "weakness in the AoC is revealed" is exactly the significance the exam wants.

### ⚠ Sharpening — not mercenaries, and mind the apostrophe

**"Wealthy people hired mercenaries"** is close but misses what makes the episode matter. Boston merchants **privately subscribed the funds** for a **state militia** of about 4,400 men under General Benjamin Lincoln — a *public* force paid for *privately*. That is the damning detail: not that rich men hired a private army, but that suppressing an armed revolt against a state government depended on a whip-round, because neither Massachusetts nor the United States could pay for it.

Congress had in fact voted to raise troops, in October 1786 — nominally to fight Native nations on the frontier, since it had no authority to intervene in a state's internal affairs. It could not fund them. Almost none were raised.

**And the name:** Daniel **Shays**, so **Shays' Rebellion**. The notes get the heading right and the possessive wrong. → [full corrections](../../CORRECTIONS.md#spelling)

## The full picture

### The squeeze

The war ended and the bills came due. Massachusetts owed money to bondholders — many of them Boston speculators who had bought soldiers' pay certificates cheaply from desperate veterans — and the state legislature, dominated by eastern commercial interests, chose to pay in full, on schedule, funded by heavy direct taxes on land and polls.

That decision landed almost entirely on western farmers, and it came with a twist that made it much worse: the taxes had to be paid in **specie** — hard coin — at a moment when almost no coin circulated in the rural west, where the economy ran on barter and credit. Farmers who could not pay were sued by creditors; those who lost were stripped of land and livestock, and could be jailed for the remaining debt.

Many of them were veterans who had themselves been paid in near-worthless certificates. They had fought a war over taxation imposed by a legislature that would not listen, and now found themselves taxed by a legislature that would not listen. They said so, explicitly and often. They petitioned for paper money, for a debt moratorium, for lower taxes, and for moving the capital out of Boston. The legislature adjourned without acting.

### The rising

**August–September 1786.** Armed crowds — calling themselves **Regulators**, a name with a long tradition of local self-help against corrupt officials — surrounded courthouses at Northampton, Worcester, Great Barrington and Concord, and prevented the courts from sitting. No court, no foreclosure. Governor **James Bowdoin** suspended habeas corpus and passed a Riot Act.

**25 January 1787.** About 1,500 men under Shays converged on the **Springfield Armory**, a federal arsenal, to seize weapons. The state militia under General **William Shepard** was already there. Warning shots were ignored; Shepard ordered artillery fired into the advancing column. **Four Regulators were killed**, twenty wounded, and the force scattered.

**February 1787.** Lincoln's privately funded militia pursued the remnant through a snowstorm and surprised them at **Petersham**. The rebellion effectively ended there. Shays escaped to Vermont.

### After

Two men were hanged. Most participants were pardoned on taking an oath of allegiance; **Shays himself was pardoned in June 1788** by Governor **John Hancock**, who had replaced Bowdoin — and Bowdoin's defeat is part of the story too. The rebels lost the fight and then won the election: voters turned out Bowdoin and much of the legislature, and the new one cut taxes and eased debt collection, doing roughly what the petitions had asked for in the first place.

### Why it changed everything

Washington, in retirement at Mount Vernon, followed the reports with alarm and wrote of them in language close to despair about whether Americans were capable of self-government. He had refused to attend the Philadelphia Convention; he changed his mind. His presence is what gave the Convention its legitimacy, and he was elected to preside over it.

More broadly, the rebellion gave the nationalists — Madison, Hamilton, Knox — the concrete example they had lacked. The argument for a stronger central government stopped being theoretical about trade statistics and became: *an armed force marched on a federal arsenal and the United States could not respond.*

### The dissenting view worth knowing

Not everyone read it as a crisis. **Thomas Jefferson**, in Paris as minister to France, wrote to Madison that a little rebellion now and then is a good thing, and that the tree of liberty must be refreshed from time to time with the blood of patriots and tyrants. He thought an occasional rising kept government honest and that the alarm was overdone.

That disagreement — is popular unrest a disease to be suppressed or a check to be tolerated? — is a live one in this course. It is the same fault line as [elite versus participatory democracy](../models_of_democracy/README.md), and it runs straight into **Federalist 10**, where Madison argues that the great advantage of a large republic is precisely its ability to contain exactly this sort of local majority faction.

**Where it sits in AP.** **Topic 1.4**, as the decisive evidence that the Articles had failed. If a free-response question asks for a specific example of the Articles' weakness, this is the strongest one available: name the rebellion, name the missing power (no authority or funds to raise a national force), and name the fix (Article I §8 gives Congress the power to tax and to call forth the militia to suppress insurrections).
