# Types of Democracy

[← Unit 1 · Foundations of American Democracy](../README.md)

**One line:** Either the people decide directly, or they elect people to decide for them — and the United States is firmly the second, with a few pieces of the first surviving at state level.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Democracy** | A system in which political authority rests with the people, exercised either by meeting and voting directly, or through elected representatives. |
| **Direct (pure)** | The people **are** the legislature. Every citizen votes on the measures themselves. Athens; New England town meetings; the Swiss *Landsgemeinde*. |
| **Representative (indirect)** | Citizens elect officials who deliberate and decide on their behalf. Also called a **republic**. This is the United States, at every level of government. |
| **The mixed devices** | **Initiative, referendum and recall** — direct-democracy tools embedded inside a representative system, available in many states and in **no** form at the federal level. |
| **Common mistake** | Filing referendum and initiative under *representative* democracy. They are direct-democracy devices; that is the whole reason they are notable. |

## From the class notes

> **DIRECT (PURE) DEMOCRACY** is a form of government when the people are the government. Every decision is voted on by the people. Incredibly ineffective, as it would take very long to make decisions about minute matters.
>
> **REPRESENTATIVE DEMOCRACY:** A small group of officials elected to represent the people. United States is a representative democracy.

Both definitions are good, and "the people *are* the government" is a sharp way to put the first one.

### ⚠ Correction — referendum and initiative are *direct* democracy

The notes give referendum and ballot initiative as the example attached to representative democracy. They belong with **direct** democracy. The giveaway is internal to the notes themselves: ballot initiatives reappear a few lines later, correctly, as the example of **participatory** democracy — and participatory is the model built on direct citizen decision-making. → [full correction](../../CORRECTIONS.md#4-referendum-and-initiative-are-direct-democracy)

They are also **three distinct devices**, and the distinction is testable:

| Device | Who starts it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiative** | Citizens, by gathering petition signatures | Puts a proposed law or state constitutional amendment on the ballot |
| **Referendum** | The legislature (*legislative referendum*), or citizens by petition (*popular referendum*) | Sends a measure to the voters — to approve it, or to repeal one already passed |
| **Recall** | Citizens, by petition signatures | Puts an official's removal to a vote before their term ends |

The notes' sentence — "the law may be proposed by a voter's initiative or by the legislature" — actually captures this correctly. It is only the heading it sits under that is wrong.

## The full picture

### Why the founders refused direct democracy

The notes' objection is the practical one: it would take forever. True, and it is the reason usually given in class. But the founders' objection was not mainly about time — it was about **faction**.

In **Federalist 10**, James Madison distinguishes a "pure democracy" from a "republic" and argues the first is *dangerous*, not merely slow. A citizen body assembled in one place can be swept by passion, and a majority that wants something unjust has nothing standing between it and doing it. His words for the record of such systems are spectacle, turbulence and contention, ending in short lives and violent deaths.

His two proposed fixes are the design of the American system:

1. **Representation** — delegating decisions to a chosen body of citizens whose wisdom may "refine and enlarge the public views", so what comes out is better than what went in.
2. **Scale** — a *large* republic. This is the counterintuitive part, and the reason Federalist 10 is a required document. Conventional theory said republics had to be small. Madison argued the opposite: a large republic contains so many competing factions that no single one can easily assemble a majority, so they must bargain and coalesce, and extreme positions get filed down.

**Brutus 1**, the Anti-Federalist reply and the other required document here, took the standard view and pushed back: a republic that large cannot represent anyone properly. Representatives will be too few and too distant, will know nothing of their constituents' lives, and will form a governing class of the wealthy and prominent. Read those two against each other and you have the entire argument of Unit 1.

### What survives of direct democracy

**At state and local level.** Roughly half the states have the initiative, referendum, or both; recall of state officials exists in about twenty. They arrived mostly in the **Progressive Era** (roughly 1898–1918), pushed by reformers who thought state legislatures had been captured by railroads and party machines and that voters needed a way around them. South Dakota was first, in 1898; California adopted the package in 1911 and uses it most heavily.

**New England town meetings** are the closest thing to real direct democracy still operating in the country — the whole eligible population of a town assembles annually to set the budget and pass local ordinances.

**At the federal level: nothing.** There is no national initiative, no national referendum, no mechanism to recall a member of Congress or a President. Not one federal question has ever been put to a nationwide popular vote. The Constitution provides no route for it, and creating one would take a constitutional amendment. This is a good fact to have ready, because it is the kind of thing people assume must exist.

### And the thing that is *not* direct democracy

The **Electoral College**. Voters do not elect the President directly; they elect electors. It is one of the clearest structural pieces of evidence for the [elite model](../models_of_democracy/README.md), and it is worth not muddling with the devices above.

**Where it sits in AP.** **Topic 1.2 (Types of Democracy)**, running straight into the three models. Federalist 10 and Brutus 1 are the required documents; the exam expects you to connect a *type* of democracy to *text* in one of them.
