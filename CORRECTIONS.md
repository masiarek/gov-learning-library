### 1. The Declaration was not a petition

**Where:** [08/26 class notes](00_Class_Notes/01_declaring_independence/README.md) → Declaring Independence.

A **petition** asks a ruler for redress and leaves his authority intact. A **declaration** announces a decision already taken. The confusion is with the **Olive Branch Petition** — adopted 5 July 1775, signed 8 July, drafted by John Dickinson — which really was addressed to George III and really did ask him to intervene. He refused even to receive it and issued the Proclamation of Rebellion on 23 August 1775. That refusal is part of *why* the tone hardened into a declaration a year later, so the two documents are worth keeping straight as a pair.

The Declaration is also not addressed to the King or to Parliament. Its stated audience is "a candid world" — it is an announcement to other nations, written to make a foreign alliance possible. The King appears in it as the *defendant*, not the reader.

**And the date.** The Second Continental Congress **adopted** the text on 4 July 1776. Most delegates **signed** the engrossed parchment on **2 August 1776**. On the 4th, the only signature generally accepted is John Hancock's as president of Congress (with Charles Thomson attesting as secretary), and even that is disputed. Two different events, four weeks apart.

→ [The Declaration of Independence](01_Foundations_of_American_Democracy/declaration_of_independence/README.md)

### 2. The Tea Party was *for* "no taxation without representation", not against it

**Where:** [08/26 class notes](00_Class_Notes/01_declaring_independence/README.md) → the three-column table.

The Sons of Liberty did not oppose their own slogan — they **organised** the Tea Party. On 16 December 1773 men associated with the group, some disguised as Mohawks, destroyed 342 chests of East India Company tea in Boston Harbor. It was civil disobedience **in defence of** "no taxation without representation", and against the Tea Act of 1773.

Worth adding, because it is the detail that makes the event make sense: the Tea Act **lowered** the price of tea. The objection was never that tea cost too much. It was that buying the cheap tea meant paying the Townshend duty, and so conceding Parliament's right to tax the colonies — plus the Act handed the East India Company a monopoly that cut colonial merchants out entirely.

→ [The Road to Independence](01_Foundations_of_American_Democracy/road_to_independence/README.md)

### 3. Natural rights are human rights

**Where:** [Democracy & the Ideals class notes](00_Class_Notes/03_democracy_and_ideals/README.md) → Ideals of Democracy.

The notes read "rights important for all humans **and animals** to have out of natural law." Drop the animals. In Locke's *Second Treatise* and in the Declaration that borrowed from it, natural rights belong to human beings by virtue of being human — the argument runs through reason, moral agency, and the capacity to consent, none of which the tradition extends to animals. Animal rights is a real and serious field of moral philosophy; it is simply a different one, and it arrives about two centuries later.

On an exam this matters because the whole point of natural rights is the *source*: rights you have **prior to** and **independent of** government, which is why a government that violates them can legitimately be replaced. Widening the category blurs that argument.

Keep: **life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness (previously property)** — that half of the note is exactly right, and the swap is a real and much-discussed edit.

→ [Ideals of Democracy](01_Foundations_of_American_Democracy/ideals_of_democracy/README.md)

### 4. Referendum and initiative are *direct* democracy

**Where:** [Democracy & the Ideals class notes](00_Class_Notes/03_democracy_and_ideals/README.md) → Democracy Review.

The example sits under **representative democracy**; it belongs with **direct** democracy. Referendum and initiative are the surviving pieces of direct democracy inside an otherwise representative American system — which is exactly why they turn up again, correctly, as the notes' example of **participatory democracy** two paragraphs later.

They are also **three distinct devices**, and the exam distinguishes them:

| Device | Who starts it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiative** | Citizens, by petition signatures | Puts a proposed law or amendment on the ballot |
| **Referendum** | The legislature (*legislative*), or citizens by petition (*popular*) | Sends a measure to the voters — to approve it, or to repeal one already passed |
| **Recall** | Citizens, by petition signatures | Puts an official's removal to a vote before their term ends |

One more fact worth having: these exist **only at the state and local level**. There is no national initiative or referendum in the United States — no mechanism exists for a nationwide popular vote on a law.

→ [Types of Democracy](01_Foundations_of_American_Democracy/types_of_democracy/README.md)

## Dates and precision

Not wrong enough to be marked wrong — but each is a place where the sharper version is easy to hold and better to write.

| The notes say | Sharper |
|---|---|
| Articles of Confederation "created in 1781" | **Drafted and adopted by Congress November 1777**; **ratified and in force 1 March 1781.** Maryland was the last holdout, over western land claims. Either date is defensible — know which one you mean, because the four-year gap is itself the story. |
| "Each state has their own constitution and Bill of Rights" | Most did. **Connecticut and Rhode Island** kept their modified colonial charters instead of writing new constitutions, and not every state attached a separate bill of rights. |
| "**State legislatures** elect delegates to send to the 2nd Continental Congress" | In 1775 they were still **colonial assemblies and provincial congresses** — they became state legislatures during 1776, as independence was declared. |
| Unitary government "may cause an oppressive king" | That is the founders' **fear**, not the definition. The UK, France and Japan are unitary *democracies*. Definition: the central government holds the sovereignty, and any local units exercise power **delegated from it** — power it can also take back. |
| Articles: "no court system" | True in the way that mattered — **no national judiciary**. Article IX did let Congress set up courts for piracy and felonies on the high seas, and act as last resort in **state-versus-state** disputes. That state-versus-state gap is the better example than "states have an issue with the fed gov". |
| "Wealthy people hired **mercenaries**" to stop Shays' Rebellion | Closer: **Boston merchants privately subscribed the funds** for a 4,400-man **state militia** under General Benjamin Lincoln. Not a private army — a public force paid for privately, because Congress could not raise one. Which is the whole point of the story. |

## Correct — in case of second thoughts

These looked checkable and check out. Do not "fix" them.

- **Boston Massacre: 5 dead, 6 wounded.** Three died at the scene, two later of wounds — five is the standard count.
- **First Continental Congress: 56 delegates, 12 of 13 colonies, Georgia absent.** All three numbers right.
- **Articles: legislation by "2/3 vote", amendment by 100%.** Nine of thirteen states for major measures (≈ two-thirds), and unanimous consent of all thirteen legislatures to amend.
- **The 17th Amendment** (1913) ended selection of senators by state legislatures — so before it, senators genuinely were not elected by the people.
- **"Life, liberty, and pursuit of happiness (previously property)."** Locke wrote property (or *estate*); Jefferson made the swap.
- **Five ideals of democracy: limited government, natural rights, popular sovereignty, republicanism, social contract.** Exactly the College Board's five.
- **Three models of democracy: participatory, pluralist, elite.** Exactly the College Board's three, and the descriptions of each are sound.
